Overview

About the course

This course provides an overview of the science of value-based decision-making: how humans learn from experience and choose among options that differ in value, probability, risk, delay and social consequence. It approaches decision-making at two levels: the careful behavioural description of choice phenomena, and their explanation through formal and computational models.

Normative benchmarks from economics and statistics serve as points of comparison against which systematic behavioural deviations are characterised and then re-interpreted, not automatically as errors but often as adaptive or resource-rational responses to the structure of the environment. The emphasis throughout is on connecting behavioural phenomena to mechanistic, computational explanations, and on keeping the normative, descriptive and mechanistic levels of analysis clearly distinct.

Syllabus

Class by class

The course is a single intellectual progression, from the foundations of choice through learning, metacognition and social decision-making, to artificial agents and applications. Click a session to expand.

1Introduction to decision-making⌄
Introduces decision-making as a multidisciplinary field studied through normative (philosophy, mathematics, economics), descriptive (psychology, behavioural economics) and engineering/AI approaches. Surveys the tools and types of data used to study decisions, and the theory-building frameworks that organise the course (aggregate versus mechanistic, descriptive versus normative), together with Marr’s three levels of analysis, of which the course concentrates on the computational and algorithmic. Sets the guiding aim: describe choice behaviour carefully, then explain it with formal and computational models.
Marr’s levelsNormative vs descriptive
2Decision-making under uncertainty⌄
From Pascal’s wager, the St Petersburg paradox and Bernoulli’s utility to axiomatic expected-utility theory, and the anomalies (mixed gambles, the fourfold pattern) that Prospect Theory accommodates through gains, losses and loss aversion. Extends the same logic from risk to time: intertemporal choice and delay discounting, exponential versus hyperbolic discounting, and the preference reversals and present bias that follow. Closes on bounded rationality and the care required when eliciting preferences over risk and delay.
Prospect TheoryDelay discountingIntertemporal choice
3Simple choices⌄
How a value-based decision is actually made once options are valued: a common currency for comparing qualitatively different options, and loss aversion as a property of that comparison. Focuses on sequential-sampling models and in particular the drift-diffusion model, developed for perceptual decisions and extended to value-based choice, where the momentary value difference is accumulated to a threshold, jointly accounting for choices and response times (including attention-weighted variants). Raises whether “simple choices” are truly simple.
Drift-diffusion modelValue comparison
4Reinforcement Learning 1⌄
Learning from experience, opening with the description–experience gap: choices among described options differ systematically from choices learned through sampling and feedback. Develops reinforcement learning from its behavioural roots in Pavlovian and operant conditioning (Pavlov, Thorndike, Skinner) to the Rescorla-Wagner model, where learning is driven by surprise. Q-learning captures state and action values, and ε-greedy and softmax formalise the exploration–exploitation trade-off. The focus stays on behavioural and computational processes.
Description–experience gapRescorla-WagnerQ-learning
5Contextual decision-making⌄
How context shapes valuation, challenging the assumption of stable, context-invariant preferences: frames, reference points, ranges and menus (asymmetric dominance and decoy effects). These effects are captured computationally by divisive normalisation and, more generally, by efficient coding: limited representational resources allocated to the statistics of the environment, so adaptation and normalisation appear as near-optimal responses to coding constraints rather than failures of rationality.
Efficient codingDivisive normalisationDecoy effects
6Reinforcement Learning 2⌄
The biases and refinements of reinforcement learning: whether learning is absolute or relative; valence-induced biases (updating more from positive than negative outcomes) and their links to risk-seeking, status-quo bias and overconfidence, including the perseveration debate. A central theme is the modulation of learning rates, asymmetrically for good versus bad news and dynamically with uncertainty and volatility, before introducing the distinction between model-free and model-based control.
Learning-rate modulationOptimism biasModel-based control
7Metacognition⌄
Metacognition as the ability to evaluate one’s own decisions, captured through confidence judgements that track difficulty and the probability of being correct. Behavioural and animal studies reveal signatures of confidence (the X-pattern, sharper accuracy at high confidence); signal detection theory separates type-1 from type-2 performance (d’ and meta-d’). Considers whether a central metacognitive process governs confidence, and the motivational, affective and computational biases that shift it, sometimes producing overconfidence.
ConfidenceSignal detection theorymeta-d’
8Social Learning⌄
How decisions are shaped by other people: social preferences (generosity, empathy, inequality aversion) that enter the utility function alongside personal payoffs. Experimental games (Ultimatum, Dictator) reveal the role of social context, identity and fairness; game theory formalises strategic interaction (Nash equilibria, coordination games, the Prisoner’s Dilemma), while humans deviate through bounded rationality and imperfect beliefs. Behavioural game theory shows how learning and experience shape social decisions, which vary across populations and contexts.
Social preferencesGame theoryFairness
9Artificial learning and decision-making⌄
Applies the behavioural and computational lens of the course to artificial agents: delegating decisions to machines, and deciding with or against a machine (algorithmic advice, algorithm aversion and appreciation). Distinguishes explicit “explainable” expert systems from opaque connectionist networks, and asks how the choices of learning agents and large language models can be studied with the same paradigms used for humans, a “machine psychology”. Examines the alignment problem, and the opacity and brittleness of current systems, with reinforcement learning as the bridge between human and machine learning.
LLMsMachine psychologyAlignment
10Applying learning and decision-making research⌄
How the science of decision-making can be used to improve decisions, contrasting three traditions of behaviour change: the behaviourist use of reinforcement and contingencies (Skinner); nudges that reshape the choice architecture while preserving freedom of choice (libertarian paternalism); and boosts that build people’s own competences, such as risk literacy. Then computational psychiatry (delusions as impaired Bayesian inference, addiction as reinforcement learning and discounting gone awry) and the transdiagnostic, dimensional view of decision parameters as continuous markers. Closes on the promise and limits of translation into health and policy.
Nudges & boostsSkinnerComputational psychiatry
11Oral presentations (two sessions)⌄
The course closes with two sessions of group presentations. Each group presents on papers of its choice connected to a course topic, and is assessed on its understanding, its critical analysis and its ability to communicate the key concepts of the course. Spreading the presentations across two Tuesdays (8 and 15 December) gives each group adequate time and lets the cohort engage with a wider range of topics.
